Five minutes now, or an awkward correction later

"Read it back" isn't the same as checking it. Here's a fast, specific way to catch the one invented number or misattributed quote before it goes out under your name — not because AI is unusually untrustworthy, but because even good models get this wrong often enough to be worth five minutes.

Last verified: September 2026

The most directly relevant benchmark isn't about chatbots in general — it's about summarizing a source document without adding facts that aren't in it. That's exactly what Loosecopy does with your notes. Vectara's open, independently-maintained Hallucination Leaderboard tests this across roughly 100 models:

1.8%best model's rate of adding unsupported claims to a summary
~10–15%where most tested models actually cluster
24.2%worst model's rate on the same task

Even at the good end, that's not zero. The review step isn't a formality — it's the only part of the pipeline actually checking whether a specific claim is true.

A fast method, adapted from digital-literacy research

Researcher Mike Caulfield built "SIFT" for evaluating anything you read online in under a minute. It maps cleanly onto checking your own AI draft:

Stop

Read the draft once as if a stranger wrote it, before you get attached to it. Don't skim for tone — read for claims: any number, name, date, or "fact"-shaped sentence.

Check the source — your source

For each claim you flagged, ask one question: did my original notes actually say this, or is this new? Anything the draft added that wasn't in what you pasted is the highest-risk category, by definition.

Find better coverage

If the draft states something as fact that you didn't provide — a statistic, an outside claim, "studies show" — a 30-second search is enough to confirm it's real or catch that it was invented. If you can't verify it quickly, cut it or soften it to an opinion.

Trace it back

For any quote or specific number, ask where it would have had to come from. If there's no plausible original source for it — including your own notes — that's usually the tell that it was generated, not recalled.

Which claims are actually worth checking

Names & titlesPeople, companies, job titles — easy to get subtly wrong or swap.
Specific numbersPercentages, dates, dollar amounts — the most common place a plausible-sounding wrong number appears.
QuotesAnything in quotation marks that you didn't paste in verbatim yourself.
"Studies show..."Any claim attributed to unnamed research or a vague authority.

The 5-minute pass

  • Every number in the draft matches a number I actually provided
  • Every name, title, or quote is one I recognize as real — not the draft's
  • Nothing is attributed to "studies" or "experts" I didn't mention
  • If I'm unsure about a claim, I cut it rather than left it in on a guess
