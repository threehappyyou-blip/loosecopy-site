Should your LinkedIn link go in the post or the first comment?

The standard advice is to never put a link in a LinkedIn post. The data is more mixed than that, and the right call depends on whether you need the reach or the click.

Last verified: November 2026

Key facts

  • Large studies find that posts with a link get fewer impressions: 18.8% fewer in Richard van der Blom's analysis of 1.3 million posts (April 2025), and 36% fewer at the median in LinkPost's 2026 study of about 358,000 posts.
  • Ordinal's data suggests the hit lands mostly on company pages, with almost no effect on personal profiles.
  • LinkedIn hasn't published what a link costs, so every figure is an observed pattern, not a rule.
  • Putting the link in the first comment tends to protect reach but loses clicks, because readers have to open the comments to find it.
  • Figures as high as 60% circulate online, but the articles quoting them don't show the data.

Does LinkedIn reduce reach for posts with links?

Probably, though by less than the scariest numbers suggest. These are the larger studies we found:

  • LinkPost (2026): about 358,000 posts. Median impressions were 448 for posts with links and 705 for posts without, a 36% gap.
  • Richard van der Blom (April 2025): about 1.3 million posts. One external link in the post body cut median reach by 18.8%. His 2026 update reported a smaller effect.
  • Ordinal (early 2023 to early 2026): a 26.5% loss across all accounts, concentrated on company pages.

These are correlations. Posts with links may also differ in other ways, such as being more promotional, so part of the gap may come from the content rather than the link itself.

Personal profile or company page: why it matters

In Ordinal's data, company pages carried most of the penalty, while personal profiles showed almost none. If you post from your own profile, a link may cost you much less than the headline numbers suggest. If you run a company page, it's worth taking the gap seriously.

Does the first-comment trick still work?

It can still protect reach, but it isn't free:

  • Ordinal's 2026 summary says it still helps company pages but costs a large share of clicks, and calls it "not the free workaround it was in 2023".
  • An older Agorapulse test (around 2019) found link-in-comment posts got 169% more impressions than standard link posts, but fewer likes and shares. It was small, six posts of each type, and the formats weren't identical.
  • Some 2026 articles claim LinkedIn now penalizes links in comments too. None of the ones we found show data for it.

Post or comment: a simple way to decide

  • When reach matters most (building an audience, a personal brand): make the post complete on its own, and put the link in the first comment or offer it to anyone who asks.
  • When the click matters most (sign-ups, sales, event registration): keep the link in the post and accept fewer impressions. Give people a reason to click in the first two lines.
  • On a company page: assume the penalty is real, and compare first-comment links with posts that carry no link at all.
  • Either way: compare your own posts in LinkedIn's analytics, and use a tracked short link so you count real clicks, not just impressions.

What about X (Twitter)?

It's less documented. Some creators report a similar drop for posts with links on X, but there's no large public study comparable to the LinkedIn ones. A common pattern is to put the link in the last post of a thread or in a reply.

Questions people ask

Does LinkedIn penalize posts with external links?

LinkedIn hasn't put a number on it, but large studies find posts with links get fewer impressions, roughly 19% to 36% fewer at the median depending on the study. The gap looks bigger for company pages.

Is it better to put the link in the first comment on LinkedIn?

It usually protects reach but loses clicks, because people have to open the comments to find it. Use it when you mainly want the post seen, and keep the link in the post when the click is the whole point.

Do company pages lose more reach from links than personal profiles?

In Ordinal's data, yes. Company pages took most of the penalty, while personal profiles showed almost none.

Does LinkedIn penalize links in comments too?

Some 2026 articles say so, but the ones we found don't show data for it. The documented cost of the first-comment approach is fewer clicks.

How can I see what links cost my own posts?

Compare impressions on similar posts with and without a link in LinkedIn's post analytics, and use a tracked short link to count clicks. Your own audience is the sample that matters most.
