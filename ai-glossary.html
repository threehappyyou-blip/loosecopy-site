Terms that come up around AI content

Short, plain-language definitions — not a full dictionary, just the words you actually run into around AI-written content, disclosure, and detection. This list only grows; nothing here gets removed, only added to or corrected.

Last updated: September 2026 · 9 terms

AI slop

Low-effort content made by AI, produced for volume rather than for the reader — the AI-era equivalent of a content mill. Researchers describe it as having "superficial competence, asymmetric effort, and mass producibility": it reads fine at a glance but has nothing underneath. The distinction from AI-assisted content is intent and oversight — one has a human directing and checking it, the other doesn't.

Source: Wikipedia — AI slop

Content Credentials (C2PA)

Often described as "a nutrition label for digital content." A technical standard (from the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) that attaches cryptographically signed, tamper-evident metadata to a file: who made it, when, what tools were involved, and whether generative AI played a part. It doesn't block or restrict the content — it just makes its history checkable.

Source: Content Authenticity Initiative — How it works

False positive / false negative

In AI-detection terms: a false positive is human-written text incorrectly flagged as AI-generated. A false negative is AI-generated text that gets waved through as human. Every detector makes both kinds of mistakes — the question is always which kind it makes more often, and to whom. See our detector reliability breakdown → for real numbers.

Hallucination

When an AI model states something false or fabricated — a quote that was never said, a source that doesn't exist, a fact that's simply wrong — with the same fluent confidence as something true. It's not the model "lying" in any intentional sense; it's a known limitation of how these systems generate text. It's also the main reason a human review step matters more than how polished a draft sounds.

Human-in-the-loop

A workflow where a person reviews, edits, or approves an AI's output before it's used or published — as opposed to a fully automated pipeline where AI output goes straight out the door. It's less a specific technology than a design choice. It's also the one design choice this entire product refuses to remove: see the pledge this is built on →.

Perplexity

A statistical measure of how predictable a piece of text is to a language model — simpler, more repetitive phrasing scores as more predictable ("lower perplexity"), while more varied or complex phrasing scores as less predictable. Most AI-text detectors lean heavily on this number. The catch: simpler, more repetitive phrasing is also common in non-native English writing, which is a documented reason those writers get falsely flagged far more often. Details on the detector reliability page →.

Provenance

The traceable history of a piece of content — who made it, when, with what tools, and what's changed since. In the AI-content world, "provenance" is increasingly the alternative strategy to "detection": instead of trying to guess whether something is AI-made after the fact, attach a verifiable record at the moment it's created. Content Credentials (C2PA) is the leading technical approach to this.

Scaled content abuse

Google's own term for its policy against mass-producing low-value pages — "many pages generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users," their own wording specifically names doing this with generative AI as an example. The target is the mass-production-without-value pattern, not the fact that a tool was involved in drafting. More on Google's actual stance on the policy breakdown page →.

Source: developers.google.com — Google Search spam policies

Synthetic media

Media — images, video, or audio — that's been wholly or partly generated or manipulated by AI, especially where it depicts a real person doing or saying something they didn't. This is the category most platform "manipulated media" policies are actually built around, and notably, most of those policies (X included) are written for images/video/audio specifically, not plain text — see how each platform actually draws that line →.
