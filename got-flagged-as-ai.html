You just got flagged. Before you do anything else, read this.

Someone — a reader, a mod, a professor, a client — just told you your writing "sounds like AI." Whether or not you used it, that message sets off a very specific kind of panic. Here's a level-headed way through it.

Last verified: September 2026
This is the page we wish had existed for us. Loosecopy exists because a founder here got a Reddit post flagged as AI-written — and the scramble that followed (was it the em dashes? the structure? do I argue or just delete it?) had no good guide to follow. This is that guide, written after having actually been there.

First, don't do these three things

  • DON'T delete the post or comment in the first five minutes. A panicked deletion often reads as an admission — even when there's nothing to admit. Give yourself a beat before you touch anything.
  • DON'T argue directly with a detector score. A percentage from a tool isn't the actual accusation — the person who flagged you is. Arguing with the number instead of the person talks past the real conversation. (And the number is shakier than it looks — here's the sourced research on why →)
  • DON'T assume the person flagging you is trying to hurt you. Most of the time they're reacting to real, sitewide fatigue with low-effort AI content. Responding like it's a personal attack usually escalates a situation that a calm reply would have closed.

What to actually do, in order

Pause before you respond

Not forever — just long enough to read the accusation once, calmly, before drafting a reply. A same-minute response is almost always worse than one written ten minutes later.

Find out what evidence they actually have

"It sounds like AI" is a vibe. "Turnitin flagged this at 94%" is a claim you can respond to. Ask which one you're dealing with — a gut reaction or an actual tool output — before you decide how seriously to take it.

A detector score alone isn't proof of anything — even the vendors say so.

Check if there's an actual rule in play

A subreddit's culture, a class syllabus, a platform's ToS, a client contract — if one of these has a specific written policy, that's what actually matters, not whether the writing "sounds like" AI. See what the major platforms actually require →

Keep your own paper trail, going forward

Notes, earlier drafts, timestamps, your own rough version before any editing — none of this helps retroactively, but it's the single best protection the next time this happens. Start keeping it now.

Take it seriously if the stakes are real

Online pushback usually blows over. A school, employer, or client accusation is a different weight class — it can turn into a real dispute fast. One Adelphi University student sued the school in 2025 after being disciplined largely on a Turnitin score, despite disputing it and citing tutoring support he'd received.

If you're in that situation, this page isn't enough — talk to someone whose job is actually that (your school's advocate office, or a lawyer who handles academic conduct cases).
Why detectors alone aren't a fair basis for a real consequence In the Adelphi case, an outside expert noted that AI-detection tools aren't consistently accurate and shouldn't be relied on without a broader evidentiary trail — not "the score said so," but the score plus context. That's a useful bar to hold any accusation to, including ones aimed at you.

If you did use AI — that's genuinely fine to say

Nothing above is about getting away with something. If AI helped draft what you posted and you edited and stand behind it, a short disclosure line closes this conversation faster than any defense does.
