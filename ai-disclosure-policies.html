AI disclosure rules, platform by platform

These policies change without much warning, so we date this page and link straight to each platform's own page — don't just take our summary, check the source if it matters for you.

Last verified: September 2026

X (Twitter)

X's official Synthetic and Manipulated Media Policy is about fabricated or simulated media — images, video, and audio depicting real people. As written, it doesn't mention AI-drafted text posts at all, and there's no disclosure requirement for text content.

Takeaway: posting a text-only thread you wrote with AI help isn't breaking a labeling rule on X — there isn't one for text. Disclosing anyway is a choice, not a requirement.

Source: help.x.com — Synthetic and manipulated media policy

LinkedIn

LinkedIn doesn't require a label on AI-assisted posts. Instead, their official guidance and user agreement put the responsibility on you: review and edit anything AI-generated before posting, make sure it's accurate, and don't share misleading information. If it's wrong, that's on you — not the AI tool.

Takeaway: no badge required, but LinkedIn is explicit that you own every word once you hit post — which is exactly why the review step matters more than the label.

Source: linkedin.com/help — Best practices for content created with the help of AI

Meta (Instagram, Threads, Facebook)

Meta's "AI Info" label is automatic, not something a creator turns on — it triggers when uploaded images or video carry AI-generation metadata (C2PA or similar tags). There's currently no version of it for plain text posts, and nothing to switch on manually for a caption or a post.

Takeaway: if all you're posting is text — which is what Loosecopy generates — this labeling system doesn't apply to you. It's built for photos and video, not writing.

Source: meta.com/help — How AI-generated content is identified and labeled

Google Search

Google has said repeatedly that disclosing AI use isn't required for ranking. What they act against is using AI to mass-produce low-value pages at scale — not the fact that a tool helped draft something. Quality, originality, and whether it actually helps the reader is what they say they evaluate.

Takeaway: disclosing AI assistance won't hurt your SEO, and hiding it won't help it — by Google's own account, it isn't a ranking signal either way.

Source: developers.google.com — Google Search's guidance on generative AI content

None of this is legal advice, and it's not a substitute for reading the platform's current terms yourself. If you're posting sponsored content, political content, or anything that might fall under a specific country's advertising or election disclosure law, that's a different question than what's covered here — worth checking with someone qualified if it applies to you.
