Getting flagged doesn't mean what you think it means

"This looks AI-generated" is a guess dressed up as a verdict. Here's what the actual research says about how good that guess is — sourced, dated, and linked so you can check it yourself.

Last verified: September 2026
This page exists because of how Loosecopy started: a founder here got a piece of writing flagged as "AI-written" on Reddit — the tool that should've existed to help with disclosure and review didn't, so we built it. That experience is the reason this page is honest about the flip side too: the tools that do the flagging are a lot less reliable than the confident percentage they show you.

Non-native English writers get flagged far more often

A Stanford study tested seven widely-used AI detectors on real TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers, alongside essays from US eighth-graders. The detectors weren't just slightly worse on non-native writing — they were dramatically worse.

61.22%of real, human-written TOEFL essays were flagged as AI-generated
97%of those essays were flagged by at least one of the seven detectors tested
~0%false-positive rate on the native-English eighth-grade essays

The researchers found the cause: detectors lean heavily on "perplexity" — how statistically predictable your word choices and sentence structure are. Simpler vocabulary and more repetitive grammar (common in non-native writing) score as more "AI-like," regardless of who actually wrote it or how.

Takeaway: if English isn't your first language, these tools are measurably more likely to flag your own writing as fake — before AI is ever involved.

Source: Stanford HAI — "AI-Detectors Biased Against Non-Native English Writers" (May 2023)

OpenAI gave up on its own detector

OpenAI — the company with the most direct financial and reputational interest in solving this — built and shipped its own AI-text classifier, then shut it down a few months later.

26%of actual AI-written text was correctly identified
9%of genuinely human-written text was incorrectly flagged as AI

It also performed worse on short passages, could be defeated by light editing, and was unreliable outside English. OpenAI's own retirement notice cited "a low rate of accuracy" as the reason.

Takeaway: if the maker of the models themselves couldn't build a detector worth trusting, a browser extension probably can't either.

Source: openai.com — New AI classifier for indicating AI-written text

Vendors self-report much better numbers than that

Not every detector is as bad as OpenAI's discontinued tool — Turnitin, for instance, states it maintains a false-positive rate under 1% on its own testing. But Turnitin's own blog post about that number is careful to add real caveats: the risk isn't zero, a score alone shouldn't be treated as proof of misconduct, and instructors are told to apply judgment and assume good faith rather than act on the score in isolation.

Takeaway: even the vendor with the best self-reported numbers tells you, in its own words, not to treat its score as a verdict.

Source: turnitin.com — Understanding false positives within our AI writing detection capabilities

Independent researchers think reliable detection may be impossible

Soheil Feizi, a computer science professor at the University of Maryland working with the NSF-funded TRAILS institute, has studied why detection keeps falling short. His conclusion is blunter than any vendor's: no publicly available detector is reliable enough for real decisions, and running AI text through a simple paraphrasing tool can knock accuracy down to roughly a coin flip.

The deeper problem: as language models keep improving, AI-written and human-written text are converging, not diverging. Feizi argues the gap the detectors are trying to measure is shrinking every year — meaning this doesn't get easier to solve over time. It gets harder.

Takeaway: this isn't a bug that better engineering fixes next year. By the researchers' own account, the underlying signal may be disappearing.

Source: TRAILS (Univ. of Maryland) — "Detecting AI May Be Impossible. That's a Big Problem For Teachers."

So what should you actually do with this?

  • Don't treat a detector score — yours or someone else's — as proof of anything. The research above says the tools themselves don't support that level of confidence.
  • Disclosure beats hoping you pass a scan. A short, honest line ("drafted with AI, edited by me") holds up regardless of what any detector says — though it's worth knowing disclosure has its own real trust costs, honestly →. Grab a ready-made line anyway →
  • The review step is what actually matters — not for fooling a detector, but because it's the difference between AI drafting and you publishing. That's the one rule we don't bend on →
  • If you were flagged and you didn't use AI (or used it and then genuinely rewrote it), you're not imagining that the system got it wrong. The research above says it does — a lot, especially if English isn't your first language.
This page is about how reliable the detection is — it isn't an argument for hiding AI use somewhere it's explicitly required (a class policy, a publication's rules, a client contract). If a specific policy requires disclosure, follow it regardless of what a detector would or wouldn't catch.
